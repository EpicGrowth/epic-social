# The field's primary claim has zero production evidence. That is the starting point.

Decentralised AI has a transparency problem, and it is not the one you might expect.

Every serious proposal for decentralised AI infrastructure leads with the same promise: transparency. Immutable ledgers, verifiable computation, auditable data pipelines. The argument is that AI governed by open protocols rather than closed corporate structures can prove what it does, how it learns, and who controls the process. It is a strong argument. It may even be the right one. But the literature does not yet support it.

The first systematic review of blockchain-native decentralised AI infrastructure screened 393 candidate papers across five major databases and retained 26 peer-reviewed studies published between 2020 and 2025. The review was pre-registered and follows the PRISMA reporting standard, the gold standard for systematic reviews in any field. The work was conducted in collaboration with researchers from the University of Auckland, King Saud University, Columbia University, and the University of Malta.

The finding that matters most: transparency is the advantage cited most frequently across the corpus. Sixty-two per cent of the reviewed papers name it. Zero provide production benchmarks to substantiate it. No latency data, no throughput measurements, no performance metrics from live deployments. The field's strongest selling point rests entirely on theoretical argument.

That is not an argument against decentralised AI. It is an argument for taking the work seriously enough to measure it.

## What "public good" actually requires when the evidence is this thin

Decentralised AI framed as a public good is not a marketing phrase. It is an obligation. Public goods require governance. They require standards. They require accountability structures that cannot be built inside a single company, a single protocol, or a single ecosystem. Calling something a public good and then leaving it to the market is not a position. It is an evasion.

The review surfaces precisely why the governance question is urgent. It identifies three barriers that any credible decentralised AI infrastructure must overcome simultaneously: scalability (current distributed ledgers cannot support AI-scale data volumes or model update frequencies), the privacy-verifiability tension (cryptographic privacy methods impose severe computational overhead at current scale), and governance accountability (no mature framework exists for assigning liability, enforcing compliance, or resolving disputes when decentralised AI systems cause harm).

These barriers are mutually reinforcing. Poor scalability forces computation off-chain, which undermines the verifiability that governance depends on. Immature governance reduces the investment incentive to solve scalability. The review names this the DeAI Trilemma: three problems that must be addressed in concert, by the constituencies they implicate simultaneously. Solving them sequentially will fail. That is the paper's sharpest analytical contribution.

Early pilot data puts a number on the current gap: approximately 100 times the latency of centralised baselines for equivalent data-processing tasks. That overhead is not a death sentence for the approach. It is the scale of the engineering and governance problem that honest measurement reveals, and it is the kind of number that disappears from pitch decks but belongs in standards conversations.

## The definitional problem underneath the evidence gap

Before you can benchmark decentralised AI, you need to agree on what it is. The review proposes a working definition: AI services whose data, model updates, and governance rules are recorded on a permissionless ledger, eliminating single-operator control.

The precision matters. Much of what currently describes itself as decentralised distributes computation geographically while concentrating control organisationally. A system that logs activity on a public ledger while a single operator retains the authority to alter models, data pipelines, or governance rules is not decentralised in any sense that matters for accountability.

Without shared definitions at this level of specificity, standards work and regulatory alignment proceed on contested ground. Every institution arriving at the governance table arrives with a different working vocabulary. A common definition is not academic housekeeping. It is the precondition for any framework that could be enforced, adopted, or funded.

## Why this cannot be solved inside the field

An evidence gap of this scale, where claims are unanimous and production validation is absent, cannot be closed by any single project or alliance. It requires a standards process: agreed metrics, shared testing methodology, transparent reporting across implementations. That work needs technical builders, governance practitioners, and capital allocators present at the same time, because the barriers they face are interlocked and the solutions must be co-designed.

This is not a theoretical observation. Practitioners building decentralised AI infrastructure have begun presenting the governance gap to intergovernmental bodies. The asymmetry between what the private sector is building and what regulators and multilateral institutions understand has been named as a structural problem at the diplomatic level. The interest is real. What has been missing is the structured forum to convert it into durable governance output.
