# AI infrastructure is already governed. The question is by whom.

*This is the first piece DeAI Summit is publishing. We're starting here — with the hardest question, because it is the question the conference is organised around. If you haven't heard of us yet: DeAI Summit convenes in Malta in Q4 2026 to bring together AI labs, decentralised AI ecosystems, and policymakers for a structured debate on AI infrastructure governance. This is what that debate is about.*

> **In this post:** Why the governance debate has stalled at the product layer. Why the infrastructure question is harder to have in public. And what a forum actually built to address it looks like.

[IMAGE: Abstract graphic representing layered infrastructure (compute, model, product) with governance applied only at the top layer]

There's a question the AI industry is very good at not answering in public: who should control the infrastructure everyone now depends on? It's a hard question because it implicates the people in the room, so most events route around it. DeAI Summit is organised around it.

The question is not abstract. The models that write, analyse, decide, and increasingly act run on compute owned by a handful of cloud providers. The training data those models consumed was curated by organisations with specific commercial interests. The evaluation frameworks that determine whether a model is "safe" were written by the same labs building the models. None of this is secret. Very little of it is governed.

## The debate that isn't happening

Most AI governance conversations are actually policy conversations: how to regulate products that already exist, built on infrastructure that is not on the table. The EU AI Act is a product-layer document. National AI strategies tend to leave compute and foundational model access implicit. The G7 and G20 AI declarations are thick with principles and thin on structural commitments.

The real centres of gravity are elsewhere. The United States is home to the majority of frontier compute. China is building parallel AI infrastructure at scale. Every other government is navigating a landscape they did not shape and may not fully understand.

That asymmetry is not a marginal fact about the AI industry. It is its defining structural feature. The governance conversation that has not yet happened is the one about whether that asymmetry is acceptable, and what alternatives actually exist.

This matters because the infrastructure layer is where the real power is. If you control the compute, you influence which models get trained. If you control the training data, you shape what models know and how they reason. If you control evaluation, you set the threshold for what reaches the public. Governing the product layer on top of an ungoverned infrastructure is like regulating the supermarket while leaving the supply chain to whoever happens to own it.

## Why this conversation is hard to have

It's hard to have in public because the people with the most informed views are the people with the most at stake:

- Frontier lab founders understand the infrastructure better than most regulators, but have a direct interest in the current structure
- Organisations building decentralised alternatives have an obvious commercial interest in making the case for them
- Capital allocators funding AI infrastructure are not neutral observers

This is not a reason to exclude any of them from the debate. It's a reason to structure the debate so positions are visible and disagreements are productive.

Most events do not do this. Panels at large conferences converge on consensus precisely because the format penalises disagreement. Closed-door regulatory consultations go the other way: highly specific, often bilateral, structurally absent of the decentralised AI voice. The result: practitioners who understand the infrastructure talk mainly to each other, and policymakers work from a partial picture. Neither group gets what they need.

## What "productive disagreement" actually means

There is a difference between a debate that performs conflict and one that produces something. The first optimises for the clip: a sharp exchange, a quotable line, applause. The second optimises for the record: what can be written down afterwards that was not true before.

DeAI Summit is built for the second. The format is deliberate:

- **Chatham House roundtables**: positions can be stated without being weaponised later
- **Direct main-stage debates**: opposed futures placed in direct conversation, not sequential keynotes that never actually meet
- **Curated seating and private dinners**: the most honest version of a disagreement usually happens in a smaller room than the one with the stage lights

A capped forum is not a smaller version of a large conference. It is a different instrument, tuned for candour rather than reach.

## Why Malta, specifically

[IMAGE: Aerial view of Malta, positioning Malta as the sovereign neutral ground]

Sovereign ground is not a detail here. An EU member state with its own functioning AI regulatory apparatus, positioned at the intersection of European, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and African markets and outside the direct orbit of the two dominant AI infrastructure powers, can host a conversation that does not belong to any single power bloc.

That neutrality is structural, not rhetorical: no participant is on home turf, and no jurisdiction's incumbents set the terms by default.

The Malta Declaration, a definite output of the 2026 summit, is intended to capture the room's productive disagreement, not a consensus engineered in advance for the press release.

## The counter-argument

The strongest version of the centralisation defence: concentration enables investment, and investment enables progress. You don't build state-of-the-art frontier models without the capital to train them at scale, and that capital concentrates because the returns are highly uncertain. Decentralised alternatives have existed for years; they have not yet produced models that compete at the frontier.

That argument deserves a real hearing, which is precisely why it needs to be made in a room that also contains the people building the alternatives, not just in the press releases of the organisations that benefit from centralisation.

## What changes

If AI infrastructure governance is taken seriously as a policy question, the structural conversations that need to happen are:

- **Who sets the conditions for compute access?**
- **Who determines what training data is acceptable, and who bears the cost of that determination?**
- **What does verifiable, decentralised model evaluation actually require?**
- **Who audits the labs' own safety functions?** Alignment and superalignment teams work on the long-term challenge of keeping AGI controllable and aligned with human values; safety systems teams are broader and product-focused, implementing model policy, handling user trust and safety, and building mitigations into deployed products. Today both answer mainly to themselves.

These questions do not have easy answers. DeAI Summit does not pretend they do. What changes at a forum like this is not the answer. It is the quality of the question, and the range of people in the room when it gets asked.
